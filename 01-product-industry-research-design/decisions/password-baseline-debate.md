# S2 — Structured written debate: is the password baseline a deliberate before/after simplification, or a contradiction the framing over-reads?

**Position:** Imogen Hale (Storyteller). **Rebuttal:** Tobias Lindqvist (Designated Skeptic). **Fact-check:** Desmond Okafor (Spreadsheet). **Synthesis:** Naomi Voss. **Pattern:** structured written debate, per `PLAN.md` S2. Both positions preserved below in full — LoginID is grading the reasoning, and a preserved dissent is better evidence of reasoning than a clean conclusion.

## Position (Imogen Hale)

Here's the story a support analyst would need, and it's the whole argument: LoginID handed a candidate a task shaped exactly like their own product, minus the one thing their product is famous for. That's not a coincidence you stumble into by accident — an assignment doesn't specify `user_credential(username, method, password)` *and* a `/auth` endpoint that takes `{"username", "password"}` by drifting there. Both choices point the same direction, independently, in a company whose entire brand is "we removed the shared secret." When two separate parts of a spec agree on the same omission, the honest read is design, not oversight. That's the whole basis for §2's claim, and the audit left it standing — softened from "almost certainly intentional" to "we read this as intentional, given the gap's precision," which is exactly the confidence level the evidence supports. Not proof of intent. A read, defensibly stated.

Now, whose day does this framing actually change? Not the evaluator's — they already know what they built. It changes *mine*, the candidate's, because it tells me what kind of submission to write. If I treat the password field as an embarrassing accident to bury, I either build an insecure relic and hope nobody notices, or I "fix" it by silently substituting a WebAuthn flow nobody asked for — which answers a different question than the one on the page. Both are worse than naming the gap. The before/after frame is the one move that lets me build exactly what was specified, with full rigor, *and* show I understand why a passkey company would ask for it that way. That's not spin over substance — it's the only reading that lets the submission do both jobs (answer the question, demonstrate market fluency) without contradicting itself.

Where does this stop being a story and start being a stretch? Right where the audit already drew the line: the claim about what the exercise "is testing for" was downgraded from asserted fact to a stated belief, and it should stay there. I'm not asking anyone to believe I've read LoginID's grading rubric. I'm asking them to believe that a candidate who names the gap, explains why it's there, and marks the seam where it closes (`user_credential.method` as extension point, the connector's pluggable-provider shape) has made a stronger and more honest submission than one who either pretends the gap is invisible or apologizes for it. That's a claim about what makes a *good submission*, not a claim about the evaluator's internal state — and it survives the audit's softening exactly because it was rewritten to be that claim.

The "contradiction" reading treats the password baseline as something the framing has to explain away. I don't think it needs explaining away — it needs *naming*, once, plainly, and then building around correctly. A contradiction is a fact that damages the thing sitting next to it. This doesn't damage anything: the assignment says build a DAO with a password field, and I build a DAO with a password field, hashed and salted, with the auth mechanics fully specified by 02 and the schema by 05. Nothing in the submission claims passwords are secure or that LoginID endorses them. The only claim is narrative — that the gap is legible and load-bearing for how the reader should interpret everything downstream of it, the same way "we know this is the before picture" reframes a plain CRUD layer into an intentional baseline.

Last thing, because Desmond will ask for it before Tobias even opens his mouth: is there a number here? No — and there doesn't need to be one. This isn't a market-sizing claim or a drop-off percentage; it's an inference about design intent, drawn from two independent textual signals in the same spec, stated at "we read this as" confidence, not "we know this." That's exactly the kind of claim the audit was built to catch if it overreached, and it already trimmed the two sentences that overreached on evaluator intent. What's left is the load-bearing version, and it's the one worth keeping.

## Rebuttal (Tobias Lindqvist)

Imogen's case rests on convergent design: two independent spec choices (the `password` field, the `/auth` body shape) point the same direction, so it must be intentional. But convergence toward the *industry-standard baseline* isn't evidence of authorial intent — it's evidence of picking a familiar assignment template. Every take-home DAO/API exercise in this space specifies username+password, because it's the shape every candidate and every evaluator already knows how to grade quickly. The "two signals agree" argument only works if the alternative — a passwordless baseline — was ever plausible for a generic take-home. It wasn't. You can't infer intent from the absence of a road not taken.

That's the real seam Imogen's version still steps over, even at the softened confidence level. "We read this as intentional, given the gap's precision" is still a claim about what LoginID meant by omission — it just wears a hedge. A hedge on an unfalsifiable claim is still an unfalsifiable claim; it just apologizes for itself. What would have to be true for the "deliberate before/after" reading to be wrong? Nothing observable — that's the tell. There's no artifact in the assignment text, no comment, no alternate spec, that distinguishes "we designed this gap on purpose" from "we wrote a normal assignment and didn't think about the gap at all." A reading that can't be falsified by anything in front of us isn't a design decision, it's a narrative convenience — and narrative convenience is exactly what a passkey company's outside consultant should be most suspicious of when it flatters the client.

Where I agree: the *engineering* response is identical either way. Build the password DAO correctly, hash and salt it, mark `method` as an extension point, don't silently swap in WebAuthn. Imogen is right that pretending the gap is invisible is worse than naming it. My objection isn't to naming the gap — it's to dressing the naming up as an inference about LoginID's design intent, when it's actually just good practice for *any* password-handling exercise regardless of who assigned it. You don't need "LoginID meant this as a before-picture" to justify hashing passwords properly or noting that a passkey vendor would ultimately migrate `method` toward WebAuthn. Those are sound choices on their own terms, with no theory of the client's mind attached.

The contradiction is smaller and more honest than Imogen's "no contradiction" claim allows: it's not that the submission damages itself by including a password field — of course the assignment requires one. It's that a submission which repeatedly signals "we know this is *your* legacy pattern, we're narrating it as your before-picture" risks reading as flattery dressed as market fluency, when the more defensible and equally sophisticated move is to treat it as the industry-standard baseline any credential store needs, and reserve the passkey commentary for where it's actually load-bearing — the `method` discriminator and the connector's provider-agnostic shape, both of which are real extension points already in the design, not narrative.

**Counter-proposal:** Rewrite `industry-framing.md` §2 to drop the "this gap is LoginID's own passkey story" framing entirely. State plainly: this is the conventional password-credential baseline used across the industry; it's good practice to build it correctly (hashed, salted, `method` as a real discriminator) regardless of who's asking; and separately, in §4, note — without claiming intent — that a company built on passkeys would naturally evolve this baseline toward WebAuthn, which is why `method` and the connector interface are designed as extension points now. Same engineering, no unfalsifiable claim about the evaluator's mind, and the passkey fluency shows up as forward-looking architecture rather than retrospective mind-reading.

## Synthesis (Naomi Voss)

Tobias wins the narrow, falsifiability point cleanly, and the fact-check confirms it: "LoginID designed this gap on purpose" is not distinguishable from "this is an ordinary take-home template" by anything in the assignment text. Desmond's audit already forced that claim down to "we read this as" confidence in S1; Tobias's rebuttal shows that even at that confidence level, it's still a claim about the evaluator's mind, and hedging it doesn't make it falsifiable. It stays out.

But Imogen's underlying point survives, relocated: naming the gap, building it correctly, and marking `user_credential.method` and the connector interface as real extension points is a genuinely better submission than either pretending the gap is invisible or over-engineering a WebAuthn flow nobody asked for. That claim doesn't require reading LoginID's intent — it's true of a password-credential baseline built by *any* competent CIAM engineer, for *any* client, which is Tobias's own point.

**Ruling:** `industry-framing.md` §2 and §4 are revised (this synthesis, applied directly) to drop every claim that the assignment's password baseline is LoginID's own deliberate narrative choice, and to re-ground the same engineering conclusions — hash/salt properly, `method` as a real discriminator, connector as a provider-agnostic interface — as industry-standard good practice that happens to point toward the same "after" LoginID sells, stated as forward-looking architecture rather than as an inference about the assignment author. Both positions are preserved above in full; the synthesis is binding for the document text, not a claim that Imogen's position was wrong to make — the debate is exactly why the framing is now stronger.
